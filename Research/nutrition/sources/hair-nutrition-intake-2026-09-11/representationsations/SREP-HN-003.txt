[paragraph:3|style:Heading 1] 1. Executive Summary
[paragraph:4|style:Normal] β-catenin is a central effector of canonical Wnt signaling and participates in hair-follicle development, stem-cell activation, and anagen entry. For nutrition, the most defensible objective is not to maximize β-catenin, but to remove nutritional, energetic, endocrine, mitochondrial, and inflammatory constraints that could impair normal follicular signaling.
[paragraph:5|style:Normal] The practical model is: adequate energy + protein/amino acids + iron/zinc/copper/magnesium + B-vitamin cofactors + vitamin D/thyroid sufficiency + essential fatty acids + redox support → better cellular capacity to execute regenerative signaling.
[paragraph:6|style:Heading 1] 2. Wnt/β-catenin and the Hair Follicle
[paragraph:7|style:Normal] Canonical Wnt signaling stabilizes β-catenin by inhibiting its normal destruction machinery. Stabilized β-catenin can enter the nucleus and interact with TCF/LEF transcription factors. In hair follicles, Wnt/β-catenin signaling is a major component of follicular development and the transition into active growth.
[paragraph:8|style:List Bullet] Wnt ligand → Frizzled/LRP5/6 → Dishevelled
[paragraph:9|style:List Bullet] Destruction-complex activity falls → β-catenin becomes more stable
[paragraph:10|style:List Bullet] Nuclear β-catenin → TCF/LEF-dependent transcription
[paragraph:11|style:List Bullet] Follicular stem/progenitor-cell activation → anagen-associated growth
[paragraph:12|style:Normal] Important nuance: more β-catenin is not automatically better. Chronic or inappropriate Wnt/β-catenin activation can be pathological. The physiological goal is appropriate timing, localization, and magnitude of signaling.

[paragraph:15|style:Normal] Tier 1 — highest-yield baseline:
[paragraph:16|style:List Bullet] CBC with indices
[paragraph:17|style:List Bullet] Ferritin
[paragraph:18|style:List Bullet] Serum iron, TIBC/transferrin, transferrin saturation
[paragraph:19|style:List Bullet] CMP (including albumin, liver/kidney function and electrolytes)
[paragraph:20|style:List Bullet] TSH and free T4
[paragraph:21|style:List Bullet] 25-OH vitamin D
[paragraph:22|style:List Bullet] Vitamin B12 and folate
[paragraph:23|style:List Bullet] Zinc
[paragraph:24|style:List Bullet] Copper and ceruloplasmin
[paragraph:25|style:List Bullet] Magnesium
[paragraph:26|style:Normal] Tier 2 — targeted metabolic/endocrine follow-up when clinically indicated:
[paragraph:27|style:List Bullet] Free T3; thyroid peroxidase antibody and thyroglobulin antibody when thyroid autoimmunity is a clinical question
[paragraph:28|style:List Bullet] Whole-blood thiamine diphosphate (ThDP/TDP) when thiamine status is specifically being investigated
[paragraph:29|style:List Bullet] Methylmalonic acid and homocysteine for selected B12/folate questions
[paragraph:30|style:List Bullet] Fasting glucose, HbA1c, fasting insulin and lipid profile to characterize metabolic context
[paragraph:31|style:List Bullet] Lactate and pyruvate when a specific mitochondrial/energy-metabolism hypothesis exists
[paragraph:32|style:Normal] Tier 3 — research/specialty testing, not routine hair-loss screening:
[paragraph:33|style:List Bullet] Plasma amino acids
[paragraph:34|style:List Bullet] Acylcarnitine profile
[paragraph:35|style:List Bullet] Urine organic acids
[paragraph:36|style:List Bullet] CoQ10/carnitine or other specialized metabolic markers
[paragraph:37|style:List Bullet] Follicular research measurements such as active/non-phosphorylated β-catenin, nuclear localization, AXIN2 and LEF1 expression
[paragraph:38|style:Normal] A serum β-catenin measurement is not a validated routine clinical test for determining whether a person's hair follicle has inadequate Wnt signaling. Follicular localization and downstream pathway activity are much more informative in research settings.
[paragraph:39|style:Heading 1] 5. Nutritional Architecture
[paragraph:40|style:Heading 2] Building materials
[paragraph:41|style:Normal] Protein; leucine-rich complete proteins; methionine/cysteine; glycine; adequate total energy.
[paragraph:42|style:Heading 2] Mitochondrial cofactors
[paragraph:43|style:Normal] B1, B2, B3, B5, magnesium, iron and other micronutrients required by oxidative metabolism.
[paragraph:44|style:Heading 2] Redox support
[paragraph:45|style:Normal] Protein/sulfur amino acids, selenium, zinc, copper, vitamins C/E and a diverse plant-food pattern.
[paragraph:46|style:Heading 2] Follicular signaling environment
[paragraph:47|style:Normal] Adequate vitamin D, protein/energy, omega-3 intake and normal endocrine/metabolic physiology.
[paragraph:48|style:Heading 2] Metabolic flexibility
[paragraph:49|style:Normal] High-quality carbohydrates as tolerated, fiber-rich plants, legumes/whole foods, healthy fats and avoidance of chronic severe energy restriction.
[paragraph:50|style:Heading 1] 6. Meal Design Rules
[paragraph:51|style:List Bullet] Make protein the anchor of each substantial meal.
[paragraph:52|style:List Bullet] Rotate salmon, sardines, other seafood, eggs, poultry, lean beef, Greek yogurt and legumes.
[paragraph:53|style:List Bullet] Pair iron-rich foods with vitamin-C-rich vegetables or citrus to improve non-heme iron absorption.
[paragraph:54|style:List Bullet] Use mushrooms, leafy greens, legumes, seeds, colorful vegetables and herbs for micronutrient and phytochemical diversity.
[paragraph:55|style:List Bullet] Use extra-virgin olive oil, avocado, nuts/seeds and fatty fish as primary fat sources.
[paragraph:56|style:List Bullet] Avoid chronic under-eating; the follicle is an energetically expensive regenerative tissue.
[paragraph:57|style:List Bullet] Do not use high-dose zinc, selenium, iron or vitamin A simply because hair is thinning; excess can be harmful.

[paragraph:66|style:Heading 1] 9. Evidence Boundaries
[paragraph:67|style:List Bullet] Strong biological evidence supports Wnt/β-catenin as an important hair-follicle signaling pathway.
[paragraph:68|style:List Bullet] Nutritional deficiencies can contribute to hair abnormalities or shedding, but the evidence differs substantially by nutrient and clinical context.
[paragraph:69|style:List Bullet] The specific human claim 'correcting nutrient X increases follicular β-catenin and regrows hair' is generally not established.
[paragraph:70|style:List Bullet] Correcting a documented deficiency is more evidence-based than deliberately forcing Wnt/β-catenin signaling upward.
[paragraph:71|style:List Bullet] Hair loss has many causes, including androgenetic alopecia, telogen effluvium, alopecia areata, thyroid disease, medication effects and inflammatory/scarring disorders; laboratory optimization should not substitute for diagnosis.
